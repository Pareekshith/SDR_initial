Step 2b · Technical Note

The NCO / Phase Accumulator

Step 2a can compute the value at any fractional position μ you hand it. This block is the piece that decides, sample after sample: is this the moment a symbol actually lands, and if so, what's μ?

What problem is this solving?

The interpolator from Step 2a is a pure function: give it four neighboring samples and a fraction μ between 0 and 1, and it hands back the signal's value at that fractional position — correctly, for any μ you ask. But it never asks "should I even bother asking this cycle?" or "what's the right μ right now?" — something else has to decide that, every single input sample, forever, while the transmitter's clock and the receiver's clock drift slowly apart.

That's this block's whole job. It watches every arriving ADC sample and keeps a running tally of "how far are we through the current symbol." Once per symbol — roughly every 4 samples, for this project's 4-samples-per-symbol target — that tally rolls over, and at exactly that moment it raises a flag (strobe) and reports exactly how far past the ideal instant the nearest real sample landed (mu_out). Feed that pair straight into Step 2a, and the two blocks together answer the real question: "what did the signal look like, right at the center of this symbol?"

The measuring-cup picture

Forget bits for a moment. Picture a measuring cup marked from empty (0) to exactly full (1 — representing one full symbol period). Every time a new ADC sample arrives, you pour in a fixed splash — always the same size, 1⁄sps of the cup's capacity (a quarter-cup splash, for this project's 4-samples-per-symbol target).

Pour four splashes and the cup is exactly full — that's the ordinary case, and it's also the moment a symbol boundary has just occurred. But cups (and accumulators) don't stop at full: the fourth splash overflows. Whatever spills over the rim, as a fraction of one splash, tells you something useful and specific — how far past that particular sample the true symbol center actually fell. Empty the cup back down to just that spillover amount, and you're ready to start filling toward the next symbol, with the overflow correctly carried forward rather than thrown away.

The two signals this produces

strobe is "the cup just overflowed" — a single-cycle pulse marking a real symbol instant, one per roughly-sps input samples. mu_out is "how much spilled over," rescaled into the units Step 2a expects (a fraction of one sample, not one cup) — feed it straight into the interpolator's mu_in and it computes the signal's value at exactly the true symbol center, not just at whatever sample happened to be closest.

From cup to bits: the real fixed-point picture

The RTL replaces the cup with a single register, phase_acc — a plain binary number where all-ones-plus-one (a full wrap, 2STEP_WIDTH) represents exactly one symbol period. Every valid input sample, it does one thing:

phase_acc ← (phase_acc + step_in + adj_in) // mod 2^STEP_WIDTH -- wraparound is automatic, free, built into fixed-width arithmetic
strobe ← carry-out of that add // did it just cross the wrap point?
mu_out ← the wrapped remainder, rescaled // see below -- just a bit-select, no real math

step_in is the "splash size" — nominally 1/sps, expressed as a fraction of 2STEP_WIDTH. For this project's real target (4 samples/symbol), that's exactly a quarter: step_in = 2STEP_WIDTH−2. adj_in is a signed trim, added in every cycle right alongside step_in — today it's tied to 0 (this sub-step tests the accumulator standalone), but it's already wired in for exactly one reason: once Step 3's Gardner loop-filter exists, its correction lands here, nudging the splash size very slightly larger or smaller every sample to speed up or slow down the count — nothing about this module needs to change when that happens.

Why mu_out is a free bit-select, not a divide

There's one genuinely subtle step, worth being explicit about: the wrapped remainder inside phase_acc is measured in symbol-period units (2STEP_WIDTH = one whole symbol), but Step 2a's mu_in needs a fraction of one sample period — a different, smaller unit. Converting between them means multiplying by sps, since one symbol period is defined as exactly sps sample periods.

Multiplying a fixed-point fraction by a power of two is free in hardware — it's just sliding the binary point, i.e. reading a different set of bits, no adder or multiplier involved at all. That's why this project fixes sps (via a LOG2_SPS parameter, default 2, meaning 4 samples/symbol — this project's real target) to a power of two: the whole "remainder × sps" rescale collapses into picking MU_WIDTH bits out of phase_acc starting a few places lower down, nothing more.

  • If adj_in stays zero, this rescale is exact — sps really is 4, and the bit-select is exactly right.
  • Once the loop filter starts trimming adj_in, the true instantaneous rate drifts a hair away from exactly 1/4 — the bit-select then rescales using the nominal sps instead of the true one, a small, standard, deliberately-accepted approximation (the correction is always tiny next to the nominal step), the same category of accepted rounding this project's interpolator already leans on for its own /6 constant-division.

Worked example, by hand

Real module defaults: STEP_WIDTH=32, sps=4 (LOG2_SPS=2), step_in = 230 = 1073741824 exactly, adj_in=0. Starting from a non-zero phase (say the accumulator already sat at 229, i.e. already half a sample-period past a boundary) shows the interesting case — not just the trivial mu=0 alignment:

At n=3, strobe=1 and the remainder is 0.125 of a symbol — rescale by sps=4 (a free 2-bit shift) to get mu = 0.5, a fraction of one sample. That's the number handed to Step 2a's mu_in: the true symbol center fell exactly halfway between two real ADC samples, and the interpolator can now compute the signal's value right there, not at either neighboring sample alone.

This isn't just theory

The real RTL (gmsk_step2b_nco.v) implements exactly this — one register, one add, one bit-select, nothing else (no multiply anywhere in this module, unlike Step 2a's Horner stages, so none of the DSP48/timing lessons from that block apply here). Its SIM gate checks it against a bit-exact Python mirror of this same fixed-point formula — not a tolerance, an exact match, since this is pure integer arithmetic with nothing analog or approximate in it — across 3000 samples spanning three phases (nominal adj_in=0, and two nonzero trims), and passed with zero mismatches on every single cycle.

Update · Sub-step B2: two crossings, one accumulator

Added 2026-08-16, once Step 2d's Gardner TED existed and needed real on-time/mid-point sample pairs, not the placeholder it was tested with. Gardner's algorithm (see that page) needs THREE interpolated samples per symbol, not one — the on-time sample, the previous on-time sample, and one taken exactly halfway between them. The interpolator can already produce that mid-point sample — it recomputes an output every single real ADC sample regardless, using whatever mu_in currently is. What was missing was something to hand it the right mu at the right cycle, twice a symbol instead of once.

The extension: watch for a second threshold crossing from the exact same accumulator. The original design (above) only ever asks "did we wrap past 1.0 this sample?" — the on-time instant. Sub-step B2 also asks "did we cross past 0.5 this sample?" — the mid-symbol instant, exactly halfway between two on-time instants by construction. Both events share the same strobe/mu_out pair, with a new is_midpoint tag saying which kind just fired.

A real mistake, caught before it became RTL

The first description of how to compute mu for the mid-point crossing (in this project's own design notes) was wrong — it proposed doubling the accumulator value and reusing the bit-select on that. It sounded right, was internally consistent when reasoned through in prose, and was written down as the plan. It was only caught by writing a script that checked it against an independently-derived floating-point answer for hundreds of crossing events before any Verilog existed — and it failed. The actual, simpler answer, below, was found by fixing that check until it passed. This is the same discipline this project's SIM gates have relied on throughout (never trust a derivation on its own merits, check it against an independent reference) — just applied one level earlier, to an architecture decision, before any code existed to test.

The actual mechanism, once corrected: the exact same bit-select gives the right μ for BOTH crossing types, completely unmodified. No doubling, no extra shift, nothing new beyond detecting that a crossing happened at all. Here's why: the mid-point μ should equal (x−0.5)×sps (how far past the 0.5 mark, rescaled to sample units) where x is the crossing-time phase. Expand that out and it's exactly the on-time formula, x×sps, minus a constant, 0.5×sps. Since sps is always a power of two (so 0.5×sps is always a whole number), that constant is an exact integer multiple of the register width μ gets truncated to — so it vanishes completely, and both formulas collapse to the identical bit pattern.

wrap_w = carry out of (phase_acc + step_in + adj_in) // on-time: crossed 1.0
mid_cross_w = !wrap_w && (old top bit=0) && (new top bit=1) // mid-point: crossed 0.5, no wrap
mu_out = new_phase[HIBIT:LOBIT] // SAME bit-select, either crossing

This isn't just theory

The real RTL (gmsk_step2b2_nco.v) implements exactly this — a new, separate module (gmsk_step2b_nco stays untouched and hardware-validated as-is). Its SIM gate checks the bit-select claim above two ways: bit-exact match against a Python mirror of the RTL's own formula (3000 samples, 3 phases, 750 on-time + 750 mid-point strobes, zero mismatches), and a from-scratch independent floating-point re-derivation of what μ should physically be at 1500 real crossing events — the same check that caught the original doubling mistake, re-run here to prove the corrected RTL, not just the corrected reasoning.

Step 2a answers "what's the value at μ?" for any μ you ask. This block decides, every sample, whether right now is worth asking, and exactly what μ to ask for — and since sub-step B2, TWICE a symbol instead of once, feeding Step 2d's Gardner TED the real on-time/mid-point pair it needs.
