Step 2e · Technical Note

The Loop Filter

The Gardner detector can tell you, once per symbol, whether the sampling instant was early, late, or about right. On its own that number does nothing — nothing in the receiver ever reads it. This is the piece that reads it, and turns it into a correction the NCO actually acts on.

What's still missing

After sub-steps A, B2 and D, the loop looks closed but isn't. The NCO drives the interpolator's mu_in, the interpolator's output feeds the TED, the TED produces e[n] — and then e[n] goes nowhere. The NCO's own step_in/adj_in ports are still just tied to a fixed constant. Every real hardware capture so far has confirmed exactly this: mu_out sits flat at 0, because the accumulator is running open-loop at a perfectly nominal, unchanging rate — nothing is telling it to do otherwise, even when e[n] is genuinely nonzero.

In real hardware, the transmitter's and receiver's clocks are two independent crystal oscillators. They are never exactly the same rate, and the starting phase between them is unknown. Left uncorrected, that mismatch doesn't stay small — it accumulates, symbol after symbol, until the sampling grid has drifted far enough that the interpolator is reading garbage. Sub-step E is the piece that closes this: it watches e[n] and continuously nudges the NCO to keep the sampling grid locked to the transmitter's actual symbol rate, indefinitely.

Why not just a low-pass filter?

e[n] is noisy — it jitters symbol to symbol even when the loop is well-locked, because real samples carry noise and the S-curve itself isn't perfectly linear away from lock. So yes, whatever reads e[n] needs to smooth it, which does sound like “low-pass filter.” But a plain single-pole smoother (a pure running average, or a leaky integrator alone) has a specific, disqualifying weakness: it can only produce a nonzero output while it's still receiving a nonzero input. Feed it a genuine, constant clock-rate offset — exactly the situation every real pair of independent oscillators is in — and a plain low-pass settles to an output that's proportional to whatever residual error is left over, never fully cancelling it. The loop would track, but with a permanent, uncorrected timing bias baked in.

What actually gets used, here and in essentially every symbol-timing and carrier-recovery loop in the literature (Gardner's own papers included), is a proportional-plus-integrator (PI) filter — two parallel paths, summed:

adj_in[n] = Kp·e[n] + integrator[n]
integrator[n+1] = integrator[n] + Ki·e[n]
  • Proportional term (Kp·e[n]) — reacts immediately to whatever the error is right now. Fast, but memoryless: the moment e[n] goes back to zero, this term contributes nothing, regardless of what's still wrong.
  • Integral term (the running accumulator) — remembers. It keeps summing every past error, so it can settle at whatever constant, nonzero value happens to exactly cancel a constant clock-rate offset — and then hold that value indefinitely, even once e[n] has been driven all the way back to zero. This is the term that gives the loop zero steady-state error against a genuine frequency offset, which a proportional-only (or low-pass-only) filter structurally cannot do.

In one line

A low-pass filter smooths noise but can't hold a persistent correction. An integrator holds a persistent correction but reacts sluggishly on its own. A PI filter is both at once — the proportional term for a fast initial response, the integral term for the zero-error lock a constant clock-rate mismatch demands.

Closing the loop

Choosing the gains: bandwidth and damping

Rather than picking Kp/Ki directly, the standard approach (Gardner 1986; also the standard second-order digital-PLL derivation used for essentially any symbol-timing or carrier loop) is to pick two much more intuitive design parameters and derive the gains from them:

  • Normalized loop bandwidth, BnT — how wide a “window” of frequencies the loop responds to, relative to the symbol rate. Small (0.005–0.02) means slow, smooth tracking that rejects noise well but takes longer to acquire lock. Larger (0.03–0.08) means faster acquisition but a noisier, more jittery locked state. This is the acquisition-speed/tracking-noise tradeoff, and it's a genuine tradeoff — there's no value that's simply “better,” only better for a given scenario.
  • Damping factor, ζ — how the loop approaches lock: under-damped (ζ<1) overshoots and rings before settling, over-damped (ζ>1) approaches slowly without overshoot. ζ=1/√2 ≈ 0.707 is the conventional default (same “maximally flat” choice used in analog Butterworth filter design) — a good compromise, not a hard requirement.

Given BnT and ζ, plus the combined gain of everything already inside the loop (the TED's own detector gain Kd — the slope of the Gardner S-curve at ε=0, the same S-curve plotted on the Step 2d page — and the NCO's gain K0, effectively 1 here since adj_in feeds the accumulator step directly), the standard result is:

θn = BnT / (ζ + 1/(4ζ))
Kp = 4ζ·θn / [(1 + 2ζ·θn + θn²) · KdK0]
Ki = 4·θn² / [(1 + 2ζ·θn + θn²) · KdK0]

Worth being upfront about

This is the standard textbook shape of the answer, not this project's final tuned numbers — Kd in particular depends on real signal amplitude and pulse shape, which we can estimate from the S-curve slope but will ultimately want to sanity-check against real hardware captures, the same way every other sub-step's constants in this project got validated against real silicon rather than trusted from a formula alone. Worked example, by hand

Illustrative numbers only (see callout above) — BnT=0.02, ζ=0.707, KdK0=1 gives roughly Kp≈0.132, Ki≈0.0018 from the formulas above. One symbol, starting from integrator=1000 (some accumulated history) and a fresh e[n]=1536 arriving from the TED:

StepComputationResult
proportional termKp × e[n] = 0.132 × 1536202.75
adj_in[n]proportional term + integrator[n] = 202.75 + 10001202.75
Ki × e[n]0.0018 × 15362.76
integrator[n+1]integrator[n] + (Ki × e[n]) = 1000 + 2.761002.76

Notice the shape of it: the proportional term dominates this symbol's output (202.75 out of 1202.75), but the integrator barely moved (1000→1002.76) — that's expected and correct. The integrator is supposed to move slowly, accumulating the small residual bias left behind after the proportional term has done its faster, coarser correction, which is exactly what lets it eventually converge on the one constant value that exactly cancels a real clock-rate offset.

Step 2a answers “what's the value at μ?” Step 2b2 decides when to ask, at two points per symbol. Step 2d measures whether that choice was right. This page is what acts on that measurement. Once this exists as real RTL, sub-step F is just wiring: this page's adj_in output into gmsk_step2b2_nco's already-present adj_in port — and the loop is genuinely, finally closed.
